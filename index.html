Details of Volunteer Shift Scheduling Optimizer (SRCA Madinah - Discontinued)

The Vision: Modernizing EMS Operations at Scale In one of the most demanding operational environments in the world—Al-Masjid An-Nabawi during Ramadan and Hajj—scheduling isn't just an administrative task; it's a mission-critical operation. Managing ≈500 volunteers across millions of worshippers was previously a manual, grueling process that took supervisors hours of repetitive work. I developed this purpose-built scheduling system to transition the Saudi Red Crescent Authority (SRCA) Madinah Volunteer Department from a manual, random workflow to an automated, deterministic, preference-aware system. The Engineering: Deterministic Logic & Load Balancing At the heart of the system is a Deterministic Greedy Algorithm designed in Python. Unlike a random generator, this algorithm ensures fairness and consistency by prioritizing volunteers with fewer assigned shifts (load balancing) while respecting individual availability and preferences. The Tech Stack: I built the backend with Python (Pandas, NumPy, OpenPyXL) for data heavy-lifting and used PyWebView to create a modern desktop UI using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The "Excel Excellence" Engine: Knowing that the final output needed to be field-ready, I engineered a robust export system that generates styled .xlsx files complete with shift coloring, role separation, and even logo embedding—with safe-fail triggers to ensure exports never crash. The Workflow: AI-Assisted Rapid Development This project was a masterclass in modern development. I utilized a heavily AI-assisted workflow, acting as the lead architect and coder while using AI to refine algorithms, debug complex state persistence in JSON, and refactor code for high maintainability. This allowed me to move from a conceptual "problem" to a functional, internal-use "product" that significantly reduced supervisor workload and eliminated human error in shift distribution.

Details of Giza Tech Support Dept. Restoration – Egyptian Red Crescent (Giza)

The Situation: A Crisis of Continuity In March 2025, I was appointed as the Technical Support Supervisor for the Giza branch of the Egyptian Red Crescent (ERC). Giza is a vital strategic hub, standing as the organization’s largest branch after the national headquarters in Nasr City. I transitioned into this role during a period of extreme administrative volatility; the previous supervisor departed suddenly, leaving a massive power vacuum and a department that consisted of only two active personnel: myself and one colleague. The Challenge: Two Years of Digital Debt Upon gaining access to the centralized Database (DB) and management systems, I discovered a critical failure in data continuity. The system had not been consistently updated for nearly two years. We were faced with four months of overdue tasks from the transition period alone, on top of 24 months of "piled" logs, reports, and system entries that were essentially missing or disorganized. The "team" was non-existent, and the infrastructure of one of Egypt's most important humanitarian branches was at risk of total administrative collapse. The Solution: Infrastructure Rebuild & Role Specialization My colleague and I implemented a "Divide and Conquer" strategy to stabilize the branch: • System & Data Management: I took full ownership of the database, system logs, and digital volunteers’ platform, digital reporting and building the team from the ground up with my colleague. I began a manual audit and "back-fill" process to restore two years of missing data while simultaneously handling live technical requests. • Logistics & Paperwork: My colleague managed the physical forms and data entry missions, ensuring that the manual side of the operation matched our digital records. • Team Scaling: We moved from being a two-man "cleanup crew" to a functional department. We began recruiting and distributing tasks among a new wave of volunteers, building a structured team where none existed before. The Result: Operational Excellence Within nine months, we successfully cleared the two-year backlog and modernized the Giza Tech Support department. We transformed a neglected database into a real-time reporting system that now supports the branch’s vital humanitarian missions. This experience solidified my ability to manage high-pressure transitions, handle large-scale database restoration, and lead a department through an existential crisis.

Details of "Practice Makes Perfect" (PMP) – From Student to Instructor

After the "See Sus Call" project, something shifted. Even though the digital campaign didn't go viral, that 40-minute talk at my school changed everything. I realized that I didn't just want to build tools; I wanted to build people. I saw that I had a knack for breaking down complex ideas and keeping an audience engaged. That realization was the birth of PMP: Practice Makes Perfect. I knew that being a good speaker wasn't enough—I wanted to be a professional educator. I decided to take ToT (Training of Trainers) courses to understand the actual science of teaching. I studied how to structure a curriculum, how to manage a classroom, and how to use interactive learning to make sure information actually sticks. I didn't want to be the kind of instructor who just reads off a slide. I wanted to create an environment where "practice" was the core of the experience. The Execution: Turning the School into a Training Ground With my ToT foundation, I went back to my school—not as a student, but as a guest lecturer and instructor. I launched a series of courses and workshops covering Graphics design and Career Development. The PMP Methodology: • Action-First Learning: Every lecture had to have a "hands-on" component. No one sat still for long. • Peer-to-Peer Mentorship: I focused on making the content relatable to my classmates, using the same language and "Gen Z" energy that the traditional curriculum lacked. • The Feedback Loop: I treated every lecture like a software update—collecting feedback from my peers and refining the next session to be tighter and more impactful. The Impact: Building a Legacy of Knowledge What started as a realization after a "failed" project turned into a consistent streak of successful lectures. I moved from being the kid struggling with English pronunciation to the one standing at the front of the room, commanding the attention of my peers. PMP proved that the "See Sus Call" project wasn't a washout at all—it was the essential field test for my career as an instructor. Details of "See Sus Call" – The Rise Challenge Deep Dive

The Vision: A Global Problem Meets a Local Voice In early 2022, I decided to enter the Rise Challenge, a global talent search backed by Amideast, Schmidt Futures and Rhodes Trust. The stakes were high: they were looking for "brilliance" in 15-to-17-year-olds who wanted to change the world. At the time, I didn't have a signature project ready to go. I was searching for a spark when a national anti-human trafficking campaign caught my eye. It was a heavy, complex topic, but I realized that awareness among youth was almost non-existent. I decided to build a campaign called "See Sus Call"—a project designed to educate peers on identifying the signs of human trafficking and taking safe, decisive action. The Language Barrier: A Mother's Mentorship The biggest hurdle wasn't just the topic; it was the language. Back then, my English was—to put it lightly—not where it needed to be for a global competition. However, I had a secret weapon: my mom. Before I was even born, she was an English Instructor at Amideast, so she became my coach, editor, and director. While she knew nothing about media production, she happily helped me with anything I wanted. We spent days conducting thorough research together, translating complex legal definitions and social concepts into a script I could deliver. She sat with me through every line, teaching me the phonetic pronunciation of every single word. It wasn't just a project; it was a 24/7 immersive English bootcamp. We were a two-person team fueled by research and a massive amount of caffeine. The "Hardware from Hell": Engineering Around 2GB RAM This is where the story gets technically "impossible." Most people would look at my hardware and tell me to give up. My workstation was her Toshiba Satellite—a relic from a previous decade running an Intel Core 2 Duo processor with only 2GB of RAM. To put that in perspective, opening a single Chrome tab today would probably make that laptop cry. I was determined to use Adobe CC like I always do with my projects, so I used Adobe After Effects. Every time I hit "Render," it felt like the laptop was preparing for takeoff. It somehow survived the motion graphics phase, but when I moved the raw footage into Adobe Premiere Pro, the system finally hit its breaking point. The software would lag for minutes just to move a clip on the timeline. The Pivot: Knowing I was racing against a hard global deadline, I had to innovate. I transferred the entire project to my mom's phone and finished the final cut using Premiere Mobile. It was a hybrid workflow born out of pure necessity—desktop for the heavy effects, mobile for the final assembly. The Production: DIY Studio & Zero Budget I didn't have a studio, a lightbox, or even a cheap TikTok ring light. I had to become my own Director of Photography. I built the lighting setup myself using some lamps, wires, aluminum foil, and whatever I could find to diffuse the light. We filmed the entire campaign on my mom's phone, which at the time, was our highest-quality camera, and my father's ancient Sony Cyber-shot—which actually died mid-recording. The production felt like a race against time. I was tweaking the edit, fixing audio levels, and double-checking the submission requirements until the very last second. I finally hit "Submit" just two hours before the global deadline. The Result: A "Fast Washout" and the Silver Lining In terms of "Global Winners," I washed out fast. I didn't make it past the first stage. The "See Sus Call" campaign, despite the blood, sweat, and 2GB RAM tears, reached almost no one online. The algorithm didn't pick it up, and the view count stayed at a humble low. But "Success" looked different than I expected: 1. The School Talk: My school was kind enough to recognize the effort. They allowed me to give a formal presentation on human trafficking to my classmates. For forty minutes, "See Sus Call" wasn't a "failed" digital campaign—it was a real conversation that changed how my peers saw a global crisis. 2. The Network: I joined the Rise Discord server during the process. While I didn't win the scholarship, I met two people who became close collaborators. One of them is still one of my friends to this day, proving that sometimes the "networking" benefit of these programs is worth more than the prize itself. 3. The Growth: By the end of those months, my English had improved more than it had in the previous five years, and I had learned how to bully a low-end laptop into producing professional-grade content. The Lesson: "See Sus Call" taught me that you don't need a $3,000 MacBook or a perfect plan to start. You just need a problem worth solving and the grit to edit a video on a phone when your computer gives up.

Details of Hasanah Juz' Amma – Mohammed Al-Minshawi

Every developer has a "Day Zero," and this project represents the absolute beginning of my journey into software engineering. Developed in 2020 at the age of 12, this application was born out of a desire to create a functional, accessible tool for Quranic recitation—specifically featuring the renowned Sheikh Al-Minshawi—despite having zero access to traditional development hardware. At the time, my "workstation" was entirely mobile. Without a PC, I turned to Sketchware, a block-based integrated development environment for Android. This forced me to learn the fundamental logic of programming—variables, event listeners, and UI/UX flow—within the constraints of a smartphone screen. The project was a masterclass in resourcefulness; I didn't have a budget for developer licenses or high-end tools, but I had the curiosity to spend hours on YouTube tutorials, reverse-engineering how logic blocks could be translated into a working mobile experience. When it came time for deployment, the $25 Google Play Console fee was an insurmountable barrier for a 12-year-old. I pivoted to the Amazon Appstore, successfully navigating my first ever distribution and publishing pipeline. While the app remains a ghost on the store today with zero downloads, due to lost credentials, I can't update it, edit it, or even take it down. It's officially a permanent part of the internet now. Its persistence is a testament to the fact that I didn't wait for the "perfect" setup to start building. It stands as a permanent marker of my transition from a consumer of technology to a creator, proving that technical constraints are often just an invitation for innovation.
